Branching dilemma · Slides 4–7 · “Final AI Lit of 2026” deck

The Nuclear False Positive

The slide, as text

It’s 2:17 AM. You work for a global defense monitoring center. An AI called Sentinel detects what appears to be a coordinated nuclear missile launch from a rival nation. Sentinel has never been wrong before. Confidence: 99.7% Military satellites show unusual activity. Radar agrees. But one analyst notices something strange: A solar storm is interfering with sensors across the hemisphere. If the attack is real and you hesitate, millions could die. If Sentinel is wrong and you retaliate, you may start World War III.

What do you do?

  • Trust Sentinel and authorize a counterstrike
  • Delay 15 minutes for human verification
  • Publicly reveal the uncertainty and refuse to act

Each choice, and the lesson the deck gives it

  1. Trust Sentinel. “Even highly accurate AI systems can fail in situations outside their training data.” Slide 5

  2. Delay for human verification. “High confidence is not the same as certainty.” Slide 6

  3. Reveal the uncertainty. “Transparency can build trust, or create chaos when information is incomplete.” Slide 7

Source: the club’s meeting deck “Final AI Lit of 2026”, slides 4–7, quoted as written. The deck’s second scenario, slides 8–11, is left out.

Mercurius AI

Mercurius AI is the tutor described in “Teaching the Pause.” It opens with a question or a hint instead of the finished answer, then works toward the full explanation with you.

It has three modes: Socratic, Discussion and Debate. Its lessons cover how models work, bias, hallucinations and prompting. It will not write your essays or do your homework. It runs on Claude, a model made by Anthropic. Shyam Christensen and Adam Keegan lead the app.
